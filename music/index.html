Entry 13 · Peel Sessions: The Drums Write the Horns Permalink

The score begins in the drum

Three hours in which percussion does not accompany the arrangement—it writes it. Afro-Bahian rhythmic cells become orchestral brass, frevo turns saxophones airborne, Afrobeat controls every horn entrance, Cuban rumba supplies the grammar, and London, Ethiopia and Japan answer in their own accents.

Field notes

The opening six tracks begin with the discovery that forced this edition into existence. SpokFrevo strikes first, but Rumpilezz immediately changes the terms: the drums are not a foundation poured before the horns arrive. They are the compositional grid. Olinda and Funmilayo carry that Brazilian argument into Afrobeat, where Tony Allen demonstrates the source code and Spok translates it back into frevo speed. The middle hour moves among Ghanaian highlife, Ethiopian melody, Cuban rumba and Nigerian/New York Afrobeat. The criterion is not simply good percussion or good brass. Each ensemble has to show causality: a drum pattern creates the space for a horn line, the horn changes the pressure on the rhythm section, and the next cycle arrives transformed. Three exact repeats surfaced during planning—one Antibalas track and a two-song Kokoroko block—and all were replaced with deeper cuts before Music population. The final hour asks how that logic survives contemporary production. Ezra Collective and Kokoroko keep it warm; Maisha creates a breathing chamber; The Comet Is Coming turns it electronic and physical; Imperial Tiger Orchestra and Akira Ishikawa bend the geography; Olinda and Spok return as compact reminders before Afrobeat, Japanese African-rock and electronic saxophone hand the sequence to Jaga Jazzist’s long-form machine. Two states enter, rhythm wins

Three hours split almost perfectly in half: Pernambuco answers Bahia across ciranda and samba chula, frevo and bloco-afro, manguebeat and soundsystem, Recife psychedelia and Recôncavo song—twenty-two rounds before Bahia takes the coda.

Field notes

The opening exchanges begin below the level of genre. Lia de Itamaracá’s ciranda answers the atabaque and close harmony of Os Tincoãs; Siba’s Zona da Mata ensemble faces the viola and call-and-response of Samba Chula de São Braz; Itamaracá’s communal circle meets the work-song memory of the Ganhadeiras. The point is not to decide which tradition is older or purer. It is to hear how two neighboring states organize bodies, voices and public space differently. Then the brass arrives. SpokFrevo and the Orquestra Contemporânea de Olinda make Pernambuco fast, angular and airborne; Ilê Aiyê and Letieres Leite make Bahia heavy with bloco-afro percussion and orchestral rhythmic cells. The psychedelic round moves from Ave Sangria and Paêbirú to deeper Antonio Carlos e Jocafi and Novos Baianos cuts. Familiar anchors are allowed to represent their states, but no exact track returns from an earlier edition. The urban round puts manguebeat’s Recife estuary against Salvador’s soundsystem. Chico Science and Mundo Livre answer BaianaSystem while Mateus Aleluia carries older Cachoeira restraint between them. Apple’s Suggested Songs supplied Sulamericano as a lead, but it survived only because it independently fit the matchup. The old songs survive the blender

Three hours of inherited music refusing museum glass: Japanese minyo becomes tropical dance music, Anatolian song grows fuzz and bass, marimba meets punk, Yoruba ritual enters Cuban rock and live electronics, and carimbó, gnawa and Kinshasa street rhythm keep finding the party.

traditional musiccumbiaminyoAnatolian folkAfro-Cubancarimbógnawaglobal dance
Field notes

The rule here is simple: tradition is raw material, not a restriction. Minyo Crusaders provide the proof of concept, carrying regional Japanese songs into cumbia, Ethiopian funk and brass-led dance music. From there the first hour keeps changing passports without changing the social contract—Mexican marimba, Anatolian saz, Amazonian carimbó, Colombian porro, Bahian community percussion and Ghanaian electronic highlife all have to work in a room full of people before they earn their place. The middle hour asks how far the transformation can go while the source remains audible. Mestre Ambrósio keeps rabeca and cavalo-marinho inside a compact band; KOKOKO! builds new instruments for Kinshasa street rhythm; Meridian Brothers programs cumbia into sly electronic geometry; Bab L’ Bluz makes guembri carry psychedelic low end; Síntesis and ÌFÉ approach Yoruba and Afro-Caribbean devotional language from different electrical generations. The aim is neither authenticity theater nor novelty tourism—the old form should still have agency inside the new machine. Nihiloxica is the deliberate pressure test: Bugandan percussion pushed toward techno intensity, placed late enough that the party has already established its trust. The final run then hands the body back from electronics to cumbia, highlife, Bahian guitar, maracatu imagination and a compact Gnawa-rock landing.
