Entry 16 · Same Hands, New Names Permalink

Every side door leads somewhere

Shared drummers, bassists, arrangers and family lines redraw sixteen established musical worlds, moving from Montreal strings through Chicago studio craft and London brass to Afrobeat, instrumental soul and a bright Brazilian ensemble finale.

Field notes

Silver Mt. Zion begins by reducing Godspeed’s scale to piano and strings; Esmerine answers with Bruce Cawdron’s mallets, cello and Turkish percussion. The path out of Grails is stranger: Lilacs & Champagne cut cinematic fragments into dusty beats, while OM lets Emil Amos ride a bass riff until repetition becomes architecture. Chicago follows through Douglas McCombs in Brokeback and the Tortoise contingent inside Isotope 217, where studio detail acquires trumpet, funk and dub space.

Lars and Martin Horntveth expose the songcraft inside Jaga Jazzist with The National Bank, then the Space 1.8 personnel web opens into London. Cykada’s James Mollison and Jake Long favor psychedelic bass and saxophone; Nérija brings Nubya Garcia and Shirley Tetteh into disciplined horn counterpoint; seed. enlarges the brass-and-drum writing. Martín Perna’s Ocote Soul Sounds and Marcos Garcia’s Here Lies Man split Antibalas into Latin flute grooves and fuzz-guitar Afrobeat before Mádé Kuti restores the family’s brass-led attack.

Hudson Whitlock takes the Surprise Chef connection into Karate Boogaloo’s raw instrumental soul. Jose Roberto Bertrami then carries Azymuth’s electric piano under his own name, and Itiberê Zwarg turns decades beside Hermeto Pascoal into playful orchestral writing. The final three songs revisit the Tortoise, Jaga Jazzist and Hermeto branches in miniature: bass and guitar, concise Norwegian pop, then a bright Brazilian resolution. Thirty-nine tracks run 2:59:59; all sixteen artist credits enter Listening Notes for the first time, with no repeated songs.

Entry 15 · Soft Air, Heavy Weather Permalink

A whisper learns to thunder

Harp, modular synths and breathy saxophone begin in near-weightlessness; over three hours, mallets, basslines, Bahian brass and Afrobeat drums gather underneath them before cello and piano clear the sky.

Field notes

Nala Sinephro opens the room slowly: harp glints around modular tones, then Pharoah Sanders enters over Floating Points’ repeating keyboard figure. Resavoir keeps the harp but brings the drums closer, while Bremer/McCoy let piano and dub-shaped bass mark the floor. By the time Tortoise’s mallets arrive, the air has acquired edges and a direction.

Portico Quartet’s ten-minute crescendo is the first real front moving in. Oren Ambarchi, Johan Berthling and Andreas Werliin reduce the same tension to guitar, bass and drums; Tortoise and Jaga Jazzist then rebuild it in brighter colours. Mildlife’s analog keys loosen the joints before Letieres Leite’s Bahian percussion makes the horns answer the drum. Seun Kuti, Antibalas and Egypt 80 turn that compositional logic into an emphatic Afrobeat release.

Kokoroko lowers the temperature through rolling percussion and unhurried brass. From there, Sven Wunder and Surprise Chef trade strings, flute, dry drums and electric keys with Azymuth’s Brazilian jazz-funk. Arthur Verocai compresses an orchestra into two miniatures; Morelenbaum² / Sakamoto, Bremer/McCoy and a final cello-piano trio leave the room nearly as quietly as it began. Where Sunday Lunch moved from samba-rock into highlife, this sequence follows arranged brass back toward stillness. Thirty-six tracks, 2:59:57, and no repeated songs.

Entry 14 · Sunday Lunch Permalink

The table has a chorus

Three warm, social hours for a family Sunday lunch: samba and salsa passing plates back and forth, samba-rock meeting son, carimbó answering timba, highlife guitar slipping between choruses, and every arrangement making room for conversation.

Field notes

This edition is designed for attention to move freely. Someone can dance, sing a chorus, argue over lunch, leave the room and come back; the music never punishes anyone for missing a structural revelation. Willie Colón opens with West African call-and-response inside New York salsa; Trio Mocotó answers in samba-rock; Celia Cruz raises the room; Antonio Carlos e Jocafi lower the temperature and keep the swing. Brazil and the Spanish-speaking Caribbean keep trading the floor instead of separating into genre blocks.

The center belongs to choruses that invite a room to join: Brasil Pandeiro, Vou Festejar, Canta Canta Minha Gente, Toda Menina Baiana, Cali Ají and A Gozar La Vida. Tim Maia’s Balanço moves from Brazilian soul into Dona Onete’s carimbó; Clube do Balanço sharpens the guitar attack; Santrofi opens that rhythm into Ghanaian highlife. Each handoff changes the accent while keeping the table communal.

The ending narrows the arrangements into quicker exchanges. Fio Maravilha and Caramba pull the guitar forward; Sufoco answers with a heavier samba chorus; A Banda do Zé Pretinho and Olere Camara loosen the coda before Toro Mata and Traigo Salsa restore brass, tumbao and collective vocals. Forty-nine tracks fill three hours to the second, with the final run resolving in bodies and voices instead of genre labels.
