Strings lower the temperature

Piano trios, flute, small-group bossa and an extended corridor of acoustic guitars turn the Brazilian instrumental thread toward daylight and open windows, preserving rhythmic detail while letting melody, decay and room sound set the pace.

Field notes

MPB, But STFU established the no-vocals rule; Still No Microphones widened the instrumentation; The Instruments Have the Floor pushed the ensembles toward brass, reeds and rapid internal change. This sequence starts by subtracting. Milton Banana Trio, Zimbo Trio and Bossa Jazz Trio trade two- and four-minute pieces, then Quarteto Novo’s remaining album cuts let flute and guitar carry Northeastern rhythm at human scale. Amilton Godoy and Edu Ribeiro narrow the frame further to piano and drums.

Ribeiro’s own group opens the middle through longer, patient development. Daniel Villa Verde, Aline Gonçalves and Gus Levy add a contemporary shimmer; The Reunion Project and Lula Galvão keep full rhythm sections moving at bossa pace, with guitar lines allowed to finish their sentences. Baden Powell then removes the band. His concise pieces begin a long acoustic corridor where Paũlo Bellinati’s Garoto repertoire and Marco Pereira’s Brazilian waltzes alternate close-miked resonance, quick modulations and carefully separated bass notes.

Toninho Horta briefly restores bass and percussion before Quarteto Maogani spreads Jobim, Guinga and Chico Buarque repertoire across four guitars. Those dry quartet voicings replace a planned clarinet-and-guitar close, making the ending more unified and less ornate. Sergio and Odair Assad reduce the room to two classical guitars: Jobim homage, Villa-Lobos miniature, choro and a songlike suite movement. Forty-four tracks run 3:00:03 in Music, with no vocals, duplicates or earlier track repeats.

Rhythm inherits the melody

Piano trios, baião guitar, electric keyboards, violin and massed woodwinds carry a third Brazilian instrumental route, moving from clipped 1960s samba-jazz toward Hermeto Pascoal’s restless ensemble logic and a maracatu-bright landing.

Open playlist in Apple Music ↗
Field notes

After the piano-trio snap of MPB, But STFU and the larger canvases of Still No Microphones, Zimbo Trio and Bossa Jazz Trio reopen the question at street level: short tunes, dry drums, bass notes placed for propulsion. Their alternating run avoids a museum-piece chronology; each group interrupts the other before Quarteto Novo’s flute and guitar pull the music northeast.

Quarteto Novo turns baião accents into elastic ensemble play, then Amilton Godoy and Edu Ribeiro reduce famous melodies to piano attack and cymbal decay. Arismar do Espírito Santo and Leonardo Amuedo keep that sparseness in two-guitar counterpoint. Chico Pinheiro restores a full rhythm section, and Cesar Camargo Mariano E Prisma push the middle toward electric keyboards before Ricardo Herz, Fabio Leandro and Pedro Ito replace voltage with violin-led coco.

The closing third keeps adding air. Vento em Madeira and Léa Freire stack reeds over Edu Ribeiro’s drums; Banda Mantiqueira answers with brass; Hermeto Pascoal’s group makes “Zurich” the long, shape-shifting summit. “Quiabo” reconnects Hermeto to Arismar, then Vento em Madeira land through darker reeds, samba articulation and the maracatu accents of “Maracatim.” Thirty-six tracks run exactly three hours in Music, with no repeats.

Still nobody needs a singer

Piano trios, regional suites, big-band choro, trombone psych, cavaquinho, violin, flute and drum-led jazz continue the argument that Brazilian melody survives perfectly well without a microphone.

Open playlist in Apple Music ↗
Field notes

Still No Microphones begins where its predecessor left off but changes the personnel completely. Tema Três and Luiz Chaves e seu Conjunto compress samba-jazz into six brisk minutes. Rodrigo Tavares opens the studio walls with electric piano and synthesizer; Trio Corrente returns to acoustic swing. Trio Curupira then spends twenty-three minutes joining regional rhythm, improvisation and suite form, preparing Vintena Brasileira’s twelve-minute orchestral “O Boi de Tatuí.”

Trombone de Frutas interrupts with a stranger rock-and-brass collage. Banda Mantiqueira answers through big-band precision, making choro breathe at orchestral scale. Mauricio Carrilho contracts the ensemble into taut Afrochoro counterpoint; Luciana Rabello and the Cazes-Gonçalves duo reduce it further to cavaquinho and seven-string guitar. Nicolas Krassik changes the lead voice to violin, then Ricardo Herz turns violin, guitar and percussion into a fast regional chamber trio.

Arismar do Espírito Santo restores low-end weight and multi-instrumental play. Léa Freire’s flute opens the final jazz section before Nene Trio makes drums the compositional engine for nearly nineteen minutes. Silvério Pontes and Zé da Velha offer a short gafieira release; Salomão Soares closes at the piano, pointing toward a younger instrumental language instead of returning to the archive. Eighteen artists, twenty-five tracks, 3:00:09: no lyrics, sung vocals, remixes, duplicates or earlier track repeats.

Primitivo becomes the compass: cinematic Brazilian psychedelia and orchestration first, then progressive composition, modern library grooves, and a personnel-led handoff into the drums and horns of the London jazz scene.

Open playlist in Apple Music ↗
Field notes

Primitivo supplies the organizing question: how can Brazilian psychedelia feel cinematic while the rhythm section remains physical? Hareton Salvanini’s orchestration opens that question through strings, guitar and dramatic studio scale. Arthur Verocai and Som Imaginário answer from adjacent composer-arranger circles, connecting MPB, progressive form and Milton Nascimento’s personnel network.

The middle moves that studio instinct outside Brazil. Mildlife lets long-form repetition carry synthesizers and jazz-funk; Misha Panfilov Sound Combo compresses cinematic atmosphere into library-groove precision; newer European and Australian groups replace orchestral sweep with patient ensemble accumulation. The arrangements remain vivid because bass, drums and keyboards keep the scene moving beneath the color.

Neue Grafik provides the final handoff through personnel rather than resemblance alone. Voodoo Rain brings Nubya Garcia’s tenor into the frame, and the network extends toward Emma-Jean Thackray’s brass, drums and vocal arrangements. The ending therefore moves from Brazilian studio composition to the London jazz scene by following players, rhythm sections and arranging methods—not by treating geography as a sequence of isolated stops.

Ten paths out from Azymuth

Three hours of electric piano, elastic bass lines and global jazz-funk, following Brazilian fusion outward through connected studios and scenes in London, Melbourne, Paris, Brussels and Tokyo.

Open playlist in Apple Music ↗
Field notes

The sequence begins close to Azymuth’s Brazilian fusion language. Grupo Medusa keeps the electric piano lyrical and the rhythm section agile; Marcos Resende & Index adds heavier synthesizer color and longer progressive development; Antonio Adolfo brings regional Brazilian rhythm into concise keyboard-led forms. Hareton Salvanini then enlarges the studio, using orchestration and psychedelic tension to make arrangement part of the drama.

Amaro Freitas shifts the emphasis from electric timbre to percussive piano, where repeated figures change weight as the trio moves. Mildlife carries patient groove into a Melbourne blend of jazz-funk, progressive rock and disco. Cortex and Marc Moulin offer two European branches: French melodic softness and Belgian studio modernism, both organized around Rhodes, bass and tightly edited horns.

Jiro Inagaki makes the Japanese branch harder and more immediate, with crisp attacks and bass lines built for movement. Neue Grafik Ensemble closes in London, where broken beat, Afro-funk, horns and guest voices turn the keyboard thread into a collaborative scene. The route widens geographically, but every stop retains the same center: rhythm-section detail, keyboards with character and arrangements that earn their duration through change.
